III.The batch

One call, many texts. A model call has a fixed cost before it reads the first word; emb spends it once for a whole batch instead of once per text.

Many texts in one call is far faster than one call each. The server does the same thing for readers who arrive together.

THE BATCH · 6 texts · — dimensions · server-side timing · measured here

WHAT YOU ARE LOOKING AT

Embedding one text is cheap; starting the work is not. The tokenizer, the matrix multiplies and the pooling each pay a fixed cost per pass, and a pass can carry many texts at once. Six texts in six passes pay it six times; six texts in one pass pay it once.

The plate measures both ways against the sandbox and draws them to scale. Both numbers are the server's own elapsed_us, bracketed on the bridge's loopback connection — execution time, with the network between you and the sandbox excluded.

WHAT JUST HAPPENED

Six passages went to the sandbox twice. First as six separate EMB commands, each its own pass over the model; then as one EMB carrying all six, which the server's batcher ran in one pass. The same six vectors come back both ways.

It is the same batcher that handles concurrent visitors: two people who ask at the same moment are coalesced into one pass whether either asked for a batch. Each run salts both sides with a fresh marker, so the measurement is never made cheaper by the cache the next plate is about.

WHY IT MATTERS

Embedding a corpus is an N-request job, and N is not small. Chunk 10,000 documents, quote every review, deduplicate every ticket: one text at a time or all of them together decides whether the job takes minutes or hours.

Batching is also why the sandbox can be small. Concurrent requests become one pass over a shared CPU, so the 2 GB machine behind this gallery serves a room full of readers without a queue.

THE EXACT COMMANDS

Both are the same command. The only difference is how many texts ride on it — and the bridge caps one call at eight.
