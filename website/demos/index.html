The demos gallery

Seventeen demonstrations of one idea, run against a real server and a real index. The first four answer what is a vector and why does it help. The next six answer why emb: several models, six languages in one space, an image beside them, and a batcher and a cache that make it cheap. Then come the two scripting plates, computing structure beside the model and answering typed questions. The last three are the zone, the same model reached over DNS from a deployment of its own; the medium, which identifies a recording by its fingerprint and searches a clip by a phrase; and the newest plate, The perception, which turns one photograph into a lit surface and measures the depth it read. Read them in order, or jump to the one you came for; each stands on its own.

Seventeen short demos, one idea: text becomes numbers, and numbers let you search by meaning. Every one runs against a real server.

HOW A RUN WORKS

The pipeline is the same on every plate. Committed text or media goes in, the model turns it into a vector in a space that model defines, the vector is compared with the rows shipped in the index, and the nearest rows become evidence for whatever the page decides next. No plate skips a stage it shows. What changes from plate to plate is the table being searched and what that evidence is allowed to prove.

THE READING ORDER

  1. 01 The vector PLATE I · Text becomes 384 numbers, and the vector is the model's answer, not a hash of the text.
    Teaches: the unit everything else is built from. ← start here
  2. 02 The similarity PLATE I · One number between two texts. Retrieval is that number, sorted.
    Teaches: the primitive retrieval is made of.
  3. 03 The search PLATE II · A question phrased in words the corpus never uses, answered with the passages that mean it.
    Teaches: the retrieval step in every RAG pipeline.
  4. 04 The atlas PLATE II · The corpus laid out by meaning. Themes here are regions of a space, not a metaphor.
    Teaches: what a vector store actually looks like.
  5. 05 The model lens PLATE III · One corpus, two models, two rankings that do not agree. You cannot mix models.
    Teaches: why shipping more than one embedding is a real decision.
  6. 06 The languages PLATE III · One multilingual model holds six languages in one space, so a translation lands where its original does.
    Teaches: one corpus, many languages, no translation step.
  7. 07 The model is a function PLATE III · One primitive, four answers: bytes, a typed vector, a similarity, a label.
    Teaches: extensibility without forking the server.
  8. 08 The image PLATE III · A photo and eight labels in one space. The model was never trained on these labels.
    Teaches: one space for two modalities; zero-shot tagging.
  9. 09 The batch PLATE III · One call carrying six texts against six calls carrying one each. The model sees one pass, not six.
    Teaches: why embedding a corpus is a job that finishes.
  10. 10 The cache PLATE III · Ask once, ask again: the same vector is memoized, so the repeat is a lookup.
    Teaches: the cheapest speed-up is the one that returns the same answer.
  11. 11 The graph PLATE IV · A Lua script builds the corpus's nearest-neighbour graph beside the model and returns edges, not a matrix.
    Teaches: the scripting layer computing structure in one round trip.
  12. 12 The decision engine new PLATE IV · Four rounds over one call: a Snake loop the model plays as a whole episode in one call, and a Pac-Man loop on the same contract with a different board (each animated beside its own live probability readout), a support inbox triaged by five typed questions, and the smallest three-question round. The typed questions answer with a real published checkpoint; the two loops run the shipped miniature.
    Teaches: the scripting layer answering typed questions, not embedding.
  13. 13 The zone new PLATE V · Every name under dns.emb.is is a sentence and every TXT record is the emoji it matches. Semantic retrieval, reached over the most boring protocol there is, on a deployment of its own. The algebra is playable: compose with what the ranking just showed you.
    Teaches: the model behind a protocol you already speak, why the record carries the answer while the working rides beside it, and what a conjunction asks that a sum cannot.
  14. 14 The medium new PLATE VI · A Shazam-style fingerprint names the exact recording from a noisy four-second excerpt, computed live in your browser; the sandbox's own model reads the same excerpt as a picture and can only describe it. Then a typed phrase finds the moment in a clip, frame by frame.
    Teaches: the trade between an exact fingerprint and an embedding, and cross-modal search over video without a second model.
  15. 15 The perception new PLATE VII · One photograph, one call. A monocular depth model answers 70 756 relative distances, and the plate reads that same reply four ways: a lit surface you can turn and cut, the map drawn as itself, the depth along a fixed slice through the middle, and a depth of field that refocuses the picture by depth, with the sharpness it achieves measured.
    Teaches: what one map is worth once it is geometry, and how far a relative, monocular ordering can be trusted before it stops being a measurement.
  16. 16 The catalog match new PLATE VIII · A real branded-food catalog drawn from two USDA releases, embedded offline and searched live. The nearest products land on a two-axis map; selecting one compares its GTIN, brand and pack with the query, and a page-local tray records the merge or review you would propose. The measured e-means-versus-Lloyd comparison runs beside it.
    Teaches: retrieval returns candidates, identity needs record evidence, and what a centroid comparison does and does not support.
  17. 17 The arrival queue new PLATE IX · A later-release product assigned to the centroids frozen in the earlier release. A cutoff explorer moves the review threshold across the held-out reference; the scatter, histogram and ECDF follow it, and the verdict labels an explored cutoff separately from the shipped p95.
    Teaches: out-of-sample assignment, reference distributions, and why review can be the honest output.

WHAT THESE ARE

Every plate embeds real text with the sandbox's own model, searches a corpus embedded before deployment, and shows the exact command and reply. Nothing is a screenshot and nothing is replayed: where a plate shows a number, it came from the server or the shipped index, and where the sandbox cannot be reached, the plate says so and shows nothing.

The corpus is Poe's collected tales and poems, 86 public-domain works from Project Gutenberg. One author's work has real thematic continents (the sea, the grave, detection, grief), which is what makes a map of the space legible. Your own text answers your request and is not stored or shown to anyone else.

The search runs in your browser over an index built offline and shipped like any other asset, so the sandbox pays one embedding per query. Nothing here writes to the sandbox, and it runs only the scripts it preloaded: EMB.EVAL is refused, on purpose. The cost plates measure the server's own timings, so "cheap" is a reading from the machine, not a claim about it.

Every plate here drives the server. The Ruby client's own surface is its companion on the other side of the wire: the same six texts, four ways of sending them, and the one rule that decides which of the four your call site is already choosing.
