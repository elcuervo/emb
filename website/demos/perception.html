VII.The perception

A photograph read by two models: one measures where everything is, the other says what looks alike. One surface comes back, with five readings — surface, wireframe, normals, contours, cloud — and a probe that finds the same patch in the other five photographs.

Depth puts the pixels in space and DINOv3 gives each patch a vector, so one photograph becomes something you can orbit, cut and search by the part you point at.

WHAT YOU ARE LOOKING AT

The stage holds one photograph and its surface: each cell of a regular grid becomes two triangles, its corners carried into camera space by the depth the model predicted. The photograph supplies the colour; a cell whose corners disagree is left out, which is where one object ends.

Everything else is a reading of the same buffers. Wireframe: every stride line and each cell's diagonal, so the topology is visible. Normals: the depth gradient's orientation, in camera space. Contours: isolines of view depth, one swept every 11 seconds. Cloud: the same vertices as points.

WHAT JUST HAPPENED

The browser bounded the sample to 768 pixels and base64-encoded it, because JSON cannot carry bytes; the bridge decoded it and the sandbox ran two graphs over the same pixels. DINOv3 answered 196 rows of 384, Depth Anything V2 a 266×266 relative map, both packed float32, both cached.

The page built the index: one grid, two triangles per cell, five depth tests per cell. One buffer feeds three renderers, so a view change is a uniform, never a rebuild. The probe is a dot product over the rows; the region grows outward from the pinned patch, not predicted.

the two calls, byte for byte

1 The browser bounds the picture: drawn to a canvas at no more than 768 pixels on the long edge, re-encoded as JPEG at quality 0.85. What leaves the page is that encode, not the file in the repository.

2 JSON carries no bytes, so the encode goes into argument 5 as base64 and bin: [5] names it; proto: 2 asks for a keyed reply. The bridge decodes argument 5 back to bytes and sends one binary-safe RESP bulk, so emb never sees base64.

3 The sandbox takes SHA-256 of the bytes first and looks up img:<model>:<sha256>:<output>, one entry per output. A miss is decoded, squashed to 224 pixels square for dinov3 — 266 for depth — rescaled 1/255 and normalised per channel.

4 One run answers every output. pooler_output and last_hidden_state leave the same forward pass, so two products cost one inference.

5 select decides what leaves the graph. none is the rank-2 pooled vector, 1 × 384. tokens with start: 5 and grid: 14 drops the CLS row and the four register rows and keeps 196 patch rows, because 196 = 14². map reduces nothing: 266 × 266, flat.

6 Both numbers come from the export's own config.json — start = 1 + num_register_tokens, grid = size / patch_size — and the server re-checks the row count against the tensor, so a wrong start is refused instead of returning a grid shifted by one patch.

7 BLOB is row-major little-endian float32, no header and no shape: 384 × 4 = 1 536 B for the vector, 196 × 384 × 4 = 301 056 B for the grid, 266 × 266 × 4 = 283 024 B for the map. The shape is read once, from EMB.INFO.

8 The bridge carries each bulk as base64, and base64 spends four characters for every three bytes: the grid and the map cross it as 401 408 and 377 368 characters. RESP framing adds a few dozen header bytes on the server side; the payload arithmetic above is what dominates the wire.

9 The second call repeats 2 to 8 against another model; the bridge takes one command at a time, so the two are sequential. The cache identity is the bytes, not the picture: the same image again is a lookup, and the same photograph re-encoded at another quality is a fresh run.

the index, the tolerances and the limits

DINOv3 was trained without captions, labels or a text tower. One run of its graph answers with two things: a pooled vector for the whole image, and a hidden state of tokens — 201 rows at 224 pixels, five reserved and the other 196 the picture itself, one vector per 16-pixel patch. emb slices the patch rows out from row 5, normalises every row, and hands the grid over as flat float32. Both products come back from the one call, so the page ranks whole pictures by the pooled vector and parts inside a picture by the grid, without a second pass.

The mesh is one regular grid at the density the reader picks, and the readout under the stage carries the vertex count of the tier drawn. A cell survives when its four edges and its diagonal each change the display depth by less than the cut tolerance, and the tolerance scales with the step, so the same surface survives at every tier: a smooth ramp changes more across a coarse step than a fine one, while a real occlusion boundary changes by the same amount either way. Refused cells leave a hole, which is the honest shape of an unknown surface.

Elevation is the rank-equalised depth times a single gain, so the exaggeration slider scales the geometry without touching the buffer. The reading, the contours, the normals and the region are uniforms over that buffer, which is why they compose: a reader can pin a patch, isolate it, ask for contours and watch the matching structures fall into different depth layers. The camera is clamped to 25° of yaw and 15° of pitch, because a single-view reconstruction has one face.

The chosen sample is sent to the sandbox for two calls; the models' weights are downloaded by the sandbox on boot, and this page ships none of them. A photograph you upload goes the same way: bounded to 768 pixels in the browser, sent as bytes, answered by the same two graphs, stored nowhere. The other five pictures are looked up once each, so the strip can rank them — one call per picture, then it is memory.

The strip asks two different questions of the replies already in hand. With no patch pinned it asks which picture is most like this one, and that is the pooled vector's cosine: 1 × 384 against 1 × 384. With a patch pinned it asks where that part comes back, which is one dot product per row of every other grid — 196 rows a picture — keeping each picture's best row and the cell it came from. Neither question sends a command.

The six photographs were not chosen by subject. Sixty-nine candidates cleared the licence filter — CC0 or public domain from Wikimedia Commons, every original at least 1800 pixels on the long edge — and each went through the plate's own depth call as EMB.IMG depth BLOB OUTPUTS depth. The 283 024-byte map was min-max normalised and reduced to the p5 and p95 spread, the share below 0.10 and above 0.90, and the gradient energy; the six kept reach both ends of the range, so the mesh has a near foreground, a middle ground and a far background to cut between. Four are CC0, two public domain, and the strip names each author.

WHY IT MATTERS

A single-view reconstruction is a shell of what one camera saw, and a shell is worth more than a picture once it is a real mesh: geometry can be measured, cut, wireframed and lit without a second model.

A vector per image answers which image; a vector per patch answers where in the image, which is where copy detection, region search and "find this part in that photo" begin. One model measures, one holds meaning, and the page says which number came from which.

what one inference buys

One pass, one space. The pooled vector is 384 wide and a patch row is 384 wide because they come out of the same graph — row 0 before pooling — so a single run yields a point for the picture and a grid for its parts in one metric. Rank whole pictures by the vector, rank parts by the grid, re-run nothing.

Those 196 rows are an index nobody had to build. normalize: true makes every row a unit vector, so a dot product is a cosine: inside a picture that is the similarity field the relief is drawn from, and between two pictures it is still a cosine, because every grid here left the same graph on the same 14 × 14 lattice at the same 384 dimensions. Six pictures are six calls, 1 176 patch vectors and 1 806 336 bytes of grid held in the page; the ranking is arithmetic over them, not a query.

The map is a different kind of output, not a wider one. select: map reduces no rows: 266 × 266 = 70 756 numbers, never normalised, declared as rows: 266, dim: 266 rather than as a grid. A dot product between two maps means nothing, so the declared shape is the whole contract and the client owns the transform.

The second graph is not free. It has its own session — EMB.INFO depth reports image_sessions: 1 — 27 MB of int8 weights on disk and about 100 MB resident once the CPU provider dequantises them, beside DINOv3's 22 MB on disk and about 90 MB resident. Both stay warm, which is why a picture already seen is a lookup instead of a second wait.

No vector database is involved. Beyond the sandbox's content-hash entries nothing is stored, the six grids live in the page's own memory, and one ranking is 196 dot products of 384 multiplies a picture. A second service would hold an index the inference already delivered.

One grid is what the five readings share: surface, wireframe, normals, contours and cloud read the same vertices and the same index, and the probe composes with any of them. The depth map is the other buffer, and the only thing the second model contributes.

THE EXACT COMMANDS

Two commands, two graphs, packed float32. BLOB is the reply form: four bytes per number, so a 196×384 grid crosses as 301 056 bytes instead of decimal text.

One row per output: the tensor it leaves, the selector that shapes it, its rows × dim and its bytes.

embedding
pooler_output · select none (rank 2) · 1 × 384 · 1 536 B
patches
last_hidden_state · select tokens, start 5, grid 14 · 196 × 384 · 301 056 B
depth
predicted_depth · select map · 266 × 266 · 283 024 B
VALUES on a map
The bridge refuses the keyword for either call; the server beneath it refuses a map's VALUES, naming BLOB and its 70 756 numbers.
a second image
Refused by the bridge: EMB.IMG takes exactly one image per call.
two products, one run
DINOv3 answers the pooled vector and the patch grid from the same forward pass. The pooled vector ranks images; the grid localises inside one.
depth is another model
A dense feature map is not a depth map: monocular depth needs a trained decoder. The plate runs Depth Anything V2 beside DINOv3 and never lets one answer for the other.
the cut is topology, not a filter
A cell is refused before any triangle exists, so the wireframe cannot draw an edge the surface does not have. The tolerance is a control — lower it and the mesh spends its triangles on the material, raise it and only real object edges open.
five readings, one geometry
Surface, wireframe, normals, contours and cloud share one vertex buffer and one index, and the probe composes with all of them. The fill rate changes, the geometry does not.
the normals are the map's
Each normal is the gradient of the depth model's own ordering, clamped so a cliff reads as a steep ramp instead of a black band. It is a convincing orientation, not a recovered surface normal.
a region is not a mask
The grown region is connectivity plus three tolerances — the model's cosine, the photograph's colour, the depth map — not a segmentation: it can leak along a wall and stop at a shadow. The box is the region's extent, not a detector's output.
one model, six pictures
The strip ranks across pictures because every grid came out of the same graph: same patch size, same 384 dimensions, so the dot products mean something. Ask a different checkpoint and the numbers are not comparable.
what it is not
No labels, no attention maps, no segmentation head, and no metric scale: the reconstruction has one face, the depth is monocular and relative, and the box is in display units.
